The hardest decision in after-hours coverage is not technical. It is deciding, in advance, which calls are worth waking someone up for. Make that decision at 2am, tired and unsure, and you will make it inconsistently. Make it once, on paper, in daylight, and your phones can follow it every night.

That is what a routing template is for. This article gives you a complete one you can copy, with the rules, the scripts, the fallback chain and the follow-up messages. It works for any home service trade, with trade-specific examples for the emergency list. If you want the cost side first, read [why every missed call is a lost job](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call).

## The principle: three outcomes, not two

Most small businesses think of after-hours as a binary: answer or voicemail. A better model has three outcomes for every call.

1. **Escalate now.** A person is notified immediately and contacts the caller.
2. **Book for the next business slot.** The caller gets a confirmed appointment or callback window.
3. **Log and follow up.** The details are captured, the caller gets a text, and the team handles it next business day.

The routing rules decide which outcome each call gets. Everything else is execution.

## The template

Copy the table below into a shared document and fill it in with your own answers. The sample entries are examples, not recommendations for your business.

| Call type | Example signals | Outcome | Who | Caller hears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Safety emergency | Smoke, fire, gas smell, sparking, active flooding, injury | Escalate now | On-call lead, then fallback | Emergency services first where appropriate, then "a person is being notified" |
| Urgent loss of service | No heat in freezing weather, no cooling for vulnerable occupants, no water, dead main circuit | Escalate now or book first slot, per your policy | On-call or dispatcher | Honest arrival window and any after-hours fee |
| Contained issue | Slow leak, partial outage, minor failure | Book next business slot | Office | Confirmed window and a text |
| Quote or estimate | Replacement, install, upgrade | Book estimate | Sales | Next available estimate slot |
| Existing customer | Warranty, reschedule, invoice | Log and follow up | Office | "We will call you at [time]" |
| Vendor or spam | Sales calls, robocalls | Log or block | None | Polite close |

### How to fill it in

Start with the emergency row, because it is the one that carries risk. Write the signals as a list a stranger could apply. "Serious leak" is vague. "Water actively entering the building and not stopped" is a rule. For a deeper look at building those lists for specific trades, see our guides to [HVAC triage](/blog/hvac-emergency-call-triage) and [electrical triage](/blog/electrical-emergency-call-triage).

## The escalation chain

An emergency call that reaches no one is worse than no promise at all. Write the chain explicitly.

:::steps
1. Notify the on-call technician by call and text.
2. If there is no response in a set number of minutes, notify the backup technician.
3. If there is still no response, notify the owner or dispatcher.
4. If all notifications fail, tell the caller honestly that the team is being reached, record a callback time and keep trying.
5. Log every escalation attempt with a timestamp for review.
:::

The number of minutes between steps is yours to choose. What matters is that it is written down, so no one improvises. With [live escalation](/features/live-escalation), the chain can be configured to bring a person into the call when the rules say so.

:::warning
Do not write a chain that depends on one person's phone. A dead battery, a tunnel or a night shift should not be able to silence your emergency line. Always have a second and third path.
:::

## Sample scripts

Scripts should be short, calm and specific. Adapt the wording to your voice.

### Opening line (after hours)

"Thanks for calling [Company]. Our office is closed, but I can help you right now. Is anyone in danger, or is this an emergency?"

### Emergency response

"Thank you. If anyone is in danger, please contact emergency services first. I am notifying our on-call technician now, and someone will call you back within [X] minutes at this number. Is it the best number to reach you?"

### Urgent but not dangerous

"I understand this is urgent. I can book you for our first available visit tomorrow morning at [time window], or we can send someone tonight for an after-hours fee of [fee]. Which would you prefer?"

### Contained or routine

"Thanks for the details. I have booked you for [day] between [window]. You will receive a text confirmation shortly. If anything changes or it gets worse, call this number again and tell us."

### Existing customer

"I can take down the details and have the office call you by [time] tomorrow. What is the best number?"

Our [call script guide](/blog/perfect-call-script-home-services) breaks down why these scripts follow the structure they do: greet, qualify, resolve, confirm.

## The confirmation text

Whatever the outcome, send a text. It turns a phone call into a record the customer can keep.

> Hi [Name], this is [Company]. We received your call about [issue] at [address]. Your visit is booked for [window]. Reply with any photos or details. If it gets worse before then, call [number].

For escalations, adapt it: "Our on-call technician has been notified and will call you within [X] minutes." See how [SMS text-back](/features/sms-text-back) fits into this flow, and keep opt-out handling in line with your own policy.

## Pricing and policy decisions you need to make first

The template depends on a handful of business decisions. Settle them before configuring anything.

- **Do you dispatch at night at all?** Some companies only escalate true emergencies.
- **What is your after-hours fee?** If you charge one, say it every time.
- **What is the on-call rotation, and how are on-call technicians compensated?** Burnout is a coverage risk.
- **What are your service-area limits at night?** You may serve a smaller radius after hours.
- **What is your cutoff time?** The latest hour you will dispatch, and the earliest you will start.

Write the answers at the top of the template so the rules below them make sense.

## Rolling it out in a week

:::steps
1. Day 1: Fill in the template with your owner, dispatcher and lead technician in one meeting.
2. Day 2: Write the emergency signals and escalation chain; have your lead approve them.
3. Day 3: Configure your phones or AI receptionist with the rules, scripts and texts.
4. Day 4: Run practice calls covering each row, including awkward and ambiguous ones.
5. Day 5: Fix what the practice calls exposed, and brief the team on-call this week.
6. Day 6 and 7: Go live, and review every after-hours call from the first weekend.
:::

If you use an AI receptionist, you can load the same table directly: the signals become triage rules, the chain becomes escalation settings and the scripts become the caller-facing wording. You can [hear a demo](/demo) with your own rules to check that it sounds right before the first real night.

## What to review after two weeks

Look at the log and ask five questions.

1. Which calls were escalated, and were they truly emergencies?
2. Which calls were not escalated that should have been?
3. How long did each escalation take to reach a person?
4. How many callers received a confirmation text?
5. Did any caller complain about what they were told?

Adjust the signals first, then the scripts, then the chain. Most problems come from signals that were too vague or too broad. Then measure the result. Our guide to [measuring your missed-call rate](/blog/how-to-measure-missed-call-rate-home-services) shows how to see whether after-hours misses are shrinking.

:::takeaways
- Use three outcomes for every call: escalate now, book for the next slot, or log and follow up.
- Write emergency signals as rules a stranger could apply.
- Build a fallback chain with at least three paths so emergencies never reach nobody.
- Say the fee and the arrival window out loud, and put them in the text.
- Send a confirmation text for every outcome.
- Review real calls after two weeks, then quarterly.
:::
