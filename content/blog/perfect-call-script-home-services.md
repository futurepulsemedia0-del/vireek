The word "script" makes a lot of business owners nervous, because a script that sounds robotic can actively drive callers away. But a good call script was never about reciting fixed sentences. It is about making sure a small set of essential questions get asked, in a sensible order, on every call, regardless of who or what is answering the phone.

This guide breaks a home service call into its parts, shows examples for each, explains common mistakes and gives you a template you can adapt.

## The four-part structure

Almost every good home service call follows the same sequence.

1. **Greet.** Name the business and offer real help.
2. **Qualify.** Learn what is needed, where, and how urgent.
3. **Resolve.** Book, escalate or give a clear next step.
4. **Confirm.** Repeat the details and tell the caller what happens next.

Everything else is detail. If a call has all four, it is a good call.

## Start with a real greeting, not a menu

The first five seconds set the tone for the entire call. A greeting that names the business and offers real help, such as "Ramirez Plumbing, this is Sarah. How can I help?", signals a live, capable conversation. A phone tree with six options signals exactly the opposite, and is often where impatient callers hang up.

### What makes a greeting work

- It names the business, so the caller knows they dialed correctly.
- It sounds like a person, not a recording.
- It invites the caller to describe the problem in their own words.
- It is short. Callers are already thinking about their problem.

After hours, add one line that explains the situation without sounding like a barrier: "Our office is closed, but I can help you right now." The [after-hours template](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) has fuller examples.

## The core qualifying questions

Four questions carry most of the weight.

:::steps
1. What service does the caller need? Get specific: "AC repair" versus "no cool" versus "strange noise" all lead to different next steps.
2. Where is the property located? This confirms it is inside your service area before anything else happens.
3. How urgent is it, and is there a safety concern?
4. What is the best way and time to reach them back?
:::

The safety question deserves special attention. For trades where hazards are real, such as gas, electrical and water, a safety screen should come early and use wording approved by your own safety lead. Our guides to [HVAC triage](/blog/hvac-emergency-call-triage) and [electrical triage](/blog/electrical-emergency-call-triage) show what that looks like.

### Additional questions by situation

| Situation | Add these |
|---|---|
| Repair | Equipment type, age, symptoms, when it started |
| Estimate or install | Scope, timeline, budget range if comfortable, photos |
| Emergency | Whether anyone is in danger, whether the source is stopped |
| Existing customer | Name, address, last service, warranty or plan status |
| Commercial | Contact role, site access, approval process |

## Know when to stop qualifying and start booking

A common mistake is over-qualifying a caller who is clearly ready to book, which frustrates them and risks losing the job to a competitor who moves faster. The best scripts recognize a ready-to-book caller early and move straight to scheduling, while still capturing enough detail for the technician to arrive prepared.

### Signals that a caller is ready

- They give the address before you ask.
- They ask "when can you come?"
- They describe the problem in specific terms.
- They sound hurried or stressed.

When you hear those, switch from asking to offering: "I can have someone there between two and four today. Does that work?"

## Resolve: book, escalate or set expectations

Every call needs an outcome. There are four.

- **Book** an appointment with a specific window.
- **Escalate** to a person for emergencies or complex cases.
- **Quote a next step** such as an estimate visit.
- **Log** the request and promise a callback at a stated time.

The worst outcome is none: the call ends and the caller does not know what happens. Be specific about timing, and keep the promise. Our guide to [measuring your missed-call rate](/blog/how-to-measure-missed-call-rate-home-services) includes a way to track how many calls end in a real booking.

:::tip
Never promise what you cannot deliver. "A technician will call you within thirty minutes" is only good if someone will. Offer a window you can meet and exceed.
:::

## Confirm: the last thirty seconds

The confirmation makes a call feel complete. Repeat the essentials:

- The address and the problem
- The booked window
- Who will arrive, if known
- Any fee the caller should expect
- A text confirmation to follow

Then send the text. With [SMS text-back](/features/sms-text-back), the caller gets a written record of the appointment, which cuts no-shows and disputes.

## A template you can adapt

Use this as a starting point and edit the language to match your voice.

> **Greeting:** "Thanks for calling [Company], this is [Name]. How can I help?"
>
> **Qualify:** "I can help with that. Can you tell me a bit about what is happening? ... And what is the address? ... Is anyone in danger, or is it getting worse right now?"
>
> **Resolve:** "I can get someone to you [window]. Would that work?"
>
> **Confirm:** "Great. I have you down for [window] at [address] for [issue]. You will get a text confirmation in a moment. Is this the best number to reach you?"

## Common script mistakes

**Reading it like a script.** Questions should sound natural. Write them as goals, then phrase them in your own words.

**Asking too many questions.** If a question does not change what happens next, drop it.

**No safety screen.** In trades with hazards, safety questions come first.

**No confirmation.** A call without a stated next step feels unfinished and invites a second call.

**Never updating.** Review real calls monthly, and fix the questions that confuse callers.

### How to prepare your script for AI

:::steps
1. Write the goals of the call: what you must learn and what outcomes are allowed.
2. List the qualifying questions in order, with the safety screen first where needed.
3. Write your service-area rules and any exclusions.
4. Define what counts as an emergency and who gets escalated.
5. Write the confirmation text.
6. Test with practice calls covering routine, emergency, angry and confusing callers.
:::

## Testing and improving your script

A script is a hypothesis about what works. Test it with real calls. Listen to ten recordings, or read ten transcripts, and mark where callers hesitated, repeated themselves or hung up. Those are the questions to reword or remove. Track two numbers over time: the share of answered calls that end in a booking, and the share of bookings that arrive with complete details. If the first is low, shorten the qualifying section. If the second is low, add one targeted question. Change one element at a time so you know what caused the improvement, and keep a simple log of versions with dates.

Share the finished script with everyone who touches the phone, including technicians who sometimes answer, so customers hear the same tone and promises whoever picks up. Revisit it each season, because the calls you get in July are not the calls you get in January.

:::takeaways
- A good script ensures the essential questions are asked in a sensible order on every call.
- Use four parts: greet, qualify, resolve, confirm.
- Start with a real greeting, not a menu.
- Ask four core questions, and add trade-specific ones.
- Recognize ready-to-book callers and move straight to scheduling.
- End every call with a clear outcome and a text confirmation.
:::
