A hailstorm does not give a roofing company a gentle ramp-up. The weather passes in twenty minutes, and by the next morning every homeowner on the affected streets is calling the same short list of contractors. Phones that rang five times a day ring fifty times. The crew that was supposed to be on a tear-off is on a roof, the office manager is on the other line, and the calls that matter most are going to voicemail.

This is the roofing version of the problem we described in [why every missed call is a lost job](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call), but it is sharper, because storm demand is concentrated into days and the callers are comparing several contractors at once. The company that answers, asks sensible questions and books an inspection slot usually keeps the job. The company that sends the caller to voicemail often never learns the job existed.

This guide covers how to handle that surge calmly: how to split true emergencies from inspection requests, which details to capture on every call, how to run follow-up when you cannot physically visit everyone, and how to prepare before the storm instead of during it.

## Why storm calls are different from normal roofing calls

On an ordinary week, a roofing call is a mix of repair requests, replacement quotes and the occasional leak. After a storm, three things change at once.

First, volume arrives in a spike, not a stream. Your phones, your calendar and your crews are all sized for the normal week. Second, callers are anxious and are usually calling several companies, so response speed carries more weight than usual. Third, the mix of calls shifts: far more people want an inspection "just to be sure," a smaller group has an active leak, and a handful are dealing with serious structural damage.

:::stat
Plan around your own history. Pull your call log for the two weeks after your last big weather event and divide by a normal two-week period. That ratio is your realistic surge multiplier, and it is a better planning number than any industry average.
:::

The practical consequence is that you cannot treat every call as an emergency, and you cannot treat every call as routine. You need a fast way to sort them, the same way [HVAC companies triage no-heat and no-cool calls](/blog/hvac-emergency-call-triage), only with roofing-specific questions.

## The three call types to separate in the first minute

Almost every storm call falls into one of three buckets. Sorting them in the first minute decides who gets a same-day response and who gets booked into the inspection queue.

### Bucket 1: Active damage that cannot wait

These calls involve water coming in now, a visible opening in the roof, a fallen tree or large debris on the structure, or sagging that suggests structural damage. They get the fastest response you can offer, which for most roofers means an emergency tarp-and-stabilize visit and a clear message to the caller about what happens next.

Questions that identify this bucket:

- Is water coming into the house right now, and where?
- Can you see daylight, a hole, or damaged decking from inside or outside?
- Did a tree, limb or other large object land on the roof?
- Is anyone in the home at risk, such as a room with a sagging ceiling?

If the caller describes a fire, an electrical hazard, or any danger to people, the right answer is to tell them to contact emergency services first. A roofing call script should say that out loud, not leave it to chance.

### Bucket 2: Leaks and damage that are contained

A stain on a ceiling, a slow drip into a bucket, or missing shingles with no active water entry. These are urgent in the sense that they will get worse, but they usually do not need a crew within the hour. They belong at the front of the inspection queue, with advice to protect belongings and photograph the damage.

### Bucket 3: "Please look at my roof"

The largest group after most storms. The homeowner heard hail, a neighbor got a claim, or an insurance adjuster suggested getting an estimate. There may be no visible damage at all. These callers need an inspection slot, a clear explanation of what the inspection includes, and a realistic wait time.

:::tip
Tell Bucket 3 callers the truth about timing. "We are booking inspections about four days out" keeps more callers than a vague "we will get to you soon," because it lets them decide for themselves instead of calling the next contractor.
:::

## What to capture on every storm call

The goal of a storm call is not to solve the roof. It is to leave your team with a complete, usable lead and the caller with a clear next step. Capture the same fields on every call, in the same order, so nothing depends on who picked up.

:::steps
1. Name, best callback number and whether texting is okay.
2. Property address, including whether the caller owns or rents the home.
3. What the caller saw or heard: hail size description, wind damage, fallen objects.
4. Whether water is entering the building now.
5. Whether they have filed, or plan to file, an insurance claim, and with whom.
6. Roof type and approximate age if they know it.
7. Photos: whether they can text pictures of the damage.
8. Booked inspection date and window, with a text confirmation.
:::

Two of those fields deserve extra attention. The insurance question shapes the conversation that follows. Some homeowners will call you before they call their insurer, some after, and some will have an adjuster already scheduled. Capture it neutrally and do not give coverage advice. The photos matter because a good set of pictures lets your estimator prioritize the queue and sometimes lets you spot a tarp job before anyone drives out.

### Keep the script honest

Storm season attracts aggressive sales tactics, and homeowners know it. A calm, honest script is a competitive advantage. Do not promise that insurance will cover the work. Do not quote a price before an inspection. Do not pressure anyone to sign on the first call. What you can promise is a specific inspection window and a clear process, and that is often enough.

## Why voicemail loses storm jobs

Voicemail fails storm callers for a simple reason: they are not waiting. A homeowner with a wet ceiling will keep dialing until a human or a competent system answers. Our comparison of [an AI receptionist and a traditional answering service](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-answering-service) goes into how consistency and speed differ, but the core point for roofers is that every minute between the first ring and the first useful question is a minute the caller spends calling someone else.

Storm surges also expose the limit of a small office. A two-person front desk can handle a normal day. It cannot handle forty calls an hour while estimators are on roofs. The roofing companies that handle storm weeks well tend to share one trait: the first ten minutes of every call run the same way, no matter how busy the office is.

## A storm-week operating rhythm

Handling the calls is half the job. The other half is keeping the queue moving so that a caller who was promised Thursday does not hear nothing until Monday.

### Before the storm

Write your triage questions down and agree on what counts as an emergency. Decide who owns the on-call phone this week. Pre-load your service area, hours and inspection process into whatever answers your phones. Set expectations with your crews about tarp capacity, because that is the first thing storms exhaust.

### During the first 72 hours

Review the call log twice a day. Move Bucket 1 callers to the top, text every booked caller a confirmation with the arrival window, and send a short "we have you scheduled" message to anyone waiting. When your inspection calendar fills, say so honestly on the phone and offer a waitlist, not a false slot.

### After the surge

Count what happened. How many calls came in, how many were answered live, how many became booked inspections, and how many of those became signed jobs? Those four numbers show where the storm week leaked, and they are the starting point for [measuring your missed-call rate properly](/blog/how-to-measure-missed-call-rate-home-services).

:::takeaways
- Storm calls spike in days, not weeks; plan from your own call history.
- Sort every call into emergency, contained damage or inspection request within the first minute.
- Capture the same eight fields on every call so any team member can pick up the lead.
- Never quote prices or promise insurance outcomes on the first call; promise an inspection window.
- Tell callers the real wait time. Honest timing keeps more leads than vague reassurance.
- Review the queue twice daily during the first 72 hours and message everyone waiting.
:::

## Mistakes roofing companies make in storm weeks

**Treating all calls as equal.** A tarp-now call and a cosmetic hail question need different responses. A single queue buries the first behind the second.

**Letting the callback list grow.** Storm callers who hear nothing for two days assume you are not interested. A short text acknowledging the request costs almost nothing and holds the lead.

**Over-promising.** Saying "we will be there tomorrow" and arriving on Friday converts a good lead into a bad review.

**Skipping the data.** If you do not log which calls were answered live and which went to voicemail, you cannot see how much storm revenue you lost. Our [cost of a missed call](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call) guide has the arithmetic for estimating it from your own numbers.

**Forgetting after-hours.** Storms often end in the evening, and the first wave of calls arrives when the office is closed. Plan [after-hours routing](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) before you need it.

## What to measure afterwards

Keep the scoreboard simple. After a storm event, record:

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Calls received vs. normal fortnight | Shows the real surge multiplier for next time |
| Share answered by a person or system on the first attempt | Shows how many leads you had a chance to keep |
| Inspections booked from answered calls | Shows how well the first call converts |
| Average wait from call to inspection | Shows whether capacity, not demand, is the constraint |
| Signed jobs from storm inspections | Shows the revenue the surge actually produced |

If the gap is between "calls received" and "answered," you have a coverage problem. If it is between "answered" and "booked," you have a script problem. If it is between "booked" and "signed," you have an estimating or follow-up problem. Knowing which one you have is worth more than any single tactic.
