Electrical calls are unusual among the home trades because the line between "annoying" and "dangerous" can be a single sentence. A customer who says "an outlet stopped working" and a customer who says "my outlet is hot and smells like plastic" are both describing a dead outlet, and only one of them needs a safety response right now.

That is why triage matters more for electricians than for almost any other trade. If the phone is answered by someone, or something, that cannot tell the difference, you either send an electrician to every nuisance call and burn your margin, or you miss a genuine hazard and carry the risk. The structure we described for [HVAC emergency triage](/blog/hvac-emergency-call-triage) applies here too: the same questions, in the same order, every time. The difference is the content of the questions and the very firm line between what a phone script should and should not say.

This guide gives you a practical triage framework you can adapt to your business, with a plain statement of where phone advice must stop.

## The one rule: the phone script protects, the electrician diagnoses

A call script is not a place for electrical diagnosis. Nobody should be walking a caller through checking a panel or resetting something when the cause is unknown. The script has three jobs:

1. Recognize signs of real danger and direct the caller to emergency services when appropriate.
2. Capture enough detail that your team can decide urgency and prepare for the visit.
3. Book the right kind of appointment and set honest expectations.

:::warning
If a caller reports fire, smoke, a person who has been shocked, or anything that sounds like immediate danger to people, the script should tell them to contact emergency services first. That instruction should come before any booking questions and should be worded by whoever is responsible for your safety and compliance policy.
:::

Everything below assumes that rule sits at the top.

## A three-tier triage model

Most electrical calls fit into one of three tiers. Agree on the definitions in writing before the busy season, because the middle tier is where disagreements happen.

### Tier 1: Safety emergency

Signs that suggest active danger:

- Burning smell, visible smoke, or scorch marks around an outlet, switch, panel or fixture
- Sparks, arcing, or a popping sound from electrical equipment
- A panel that is hot to the touch, buzzing, or has a breaker that will not stay reset alongside any of the above
- Exposed, damaged or downed wires
- Water reaching outlets, panels or fixtures
- Anyone injured or shocked

For these calls, the response is emergency services where appropriate, immediate escalation to your on-call electrician, and a clear message that the caller should stay away from the affected area until help arrives. How far your script goes beyond that is a policy decision for your business and your insurer, not a copywriting choice.

### Tier 2: Urgent loss of function

Power is out in part of the home, a circuit is dead, or a major appliance circuit has failed, but there are no danger signs. The customer needs help soon, and some of these will be same-day. The script should ask the diagnostic questions in the next section and route according to your own rules for after-hours work.

### Tier 3: Scheduled work

Panel upgrades, EV charger installs, lighting, ceiling fans, rewiring, inspections, outlet additions. These are quote-and-book calls. Capturing the details well here is what turns a phone call into a clean estimate.

:::tip
Write the Tier 1 list on one page and have your licensed lead sign it off. A triage script is only as good as the definition behind it, and "someone signed off on this" is an excellent answer when a customer or insurer asks.
:::

## The questions that sort a call in under a minute

You do not need many questions. You need the right few, asked in a fixed order.

:::steps
1. "Is anyone in danger right now, or do you see smoke, flames or sparks?"
2. "Do you smell anything burning or see scorch marks?"
3. "Is there any water near the outlet, panel or fixture?"
4. "What exactly stopped working, and when did it start?"
5. "Is it one outlet or fixture, one room, or the whole home?"
6. "Has a breaker tripped, and does it stay reset?"
7. "What is the property address, and is there a safe place for us to park?"
8. "What is the best number to reach you, and can we text you?"
:::

The first three questions are the safety screen. If any answer is yes, the call moves to Tier 1 handling immediately. The next three narrow the scope of the problem. The last two are logistics. Notice what is missing: nothing in that list asks the caller to touch, test or open anything.

### Why the same order matters

A human dispatcher who has been up since 4am will not ask questions in the same order at 2am. An AI receptionist will, which is the main reason [consistency matters more than raw talent](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-answering-service) in triage. If the safety screen is always the first thing asked, it cannot be skipped by accident.

## What to capture for each type of work

A good intake record saves the estimator a trip and saves the customer a second explanation.

| Job type | Details to capture |
|---|---|
| Dead outlet or circuit | Location, how many affected, breaker status, any recent work or appliance changes |
| Panel issue or upgrade | Panel brand and age if visible, reason for the call, planned additions |
| EV charger | Vehicle and charger model, garage or driveway, distance to panel |
| Lighting or fan | Room, ceiling height, existing wiring or new run, fixture supplied or not |
| Whole-home outage | Whether neighbors are affected, whether the utility is involved, any storm |
| Inspection or safety check | Reason (sale, insurance, concern), property age, known issues |

Photos help enormously for panels and EV installs. Asking the caller to text a photo of the panel door label, not the inside of the panel, gives your estimator what they need without asking the customer to open anything.

## Handling the utility versus your job

A frequent source of wasted dispatches is the whole-home outage that turns out to be a utility problem. A short qualifying question helps: are neighbors also without power, and is there a known outage? If the answer is yes, the right response is to point the caller to their utility and offer to follow up if the issue remains after power returns. That is honest, it saves a truck roll, and it earns trust. Our guide to [what a lost or wasted call really costs](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call) covers the margin math in a related setting.

## After-hours electrical calls

Electricians face a sharper after-hours question than most trades. Do you answer at all? Do you dispatch? Do you charge a premium? Those are business decisions, and the phone setup should reflect them rather than improvise.

A workable pattern:

- **Tier 1 calls** reach your on-call electrician immediately, and the caller is told to contact emergency services when appropriate.
- **Tier 2 calls** are taken, qualified and offered either an after-hours premium visit or the first slot the next morning, according to your policy.
- **Tier 3 calls** are logged and booked into the normal quote queue.

The [after-hours routing template](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) lays out how to write that logic down so it can be configured once and followed every time. With [live escalation](/features/live-escalation), the Tier 1 calls can reach a person while the rest follow the booking path.

## Where an AI receptionist helps, and where it should not

An AI receptionist is well suited to the repeatable parts of this process: answering every call, asking the safety screen first, capturing the intake fields, booking the visit type and escalating Tier 1 calls to a human. It should not diagnose, advise on repairs or reassure a caller that a hazard is "probably fine." Configure the boundaries with your licensed lead and test them with scripted practice calls, including awkward ones.

:::takeaways
- The phone script protects and routes; the electrician diagnoses.
- Put a safety screen first on every call and direct danger signs to emergency services.
- Use three tiers: safety emergency, urgent loss of function, scheduled work.
- Ask a fixed set of questions in a fixed order, and never ask a caller to touch or test anything.
- Capture job-specific details so estimators arrive prepared.
- Decide your after-hours policy in writing before the busy season.
:::

## A short checklist for rolling this out

1. Have your licensed lead write and approve the Tier 1 list.
2. Draft the eight triage questions and the first-line safety statement.
3. Decide your after-hours policy for each tier, including pricing.
4. Set up escalation so Tier 1 calls reach a person.
5. Run ten practice calls, including at least three that try to confuse the script.
6. Review the first week of real calls and adjust the wording.

Done well, triage does not slow the call down. It makes the call faster, because both sides know the next step by the end of the first minute. And it gives you a record you can stand behind.
