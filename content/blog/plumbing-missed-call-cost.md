Plumbing calls skew more urgent than almost any other home service trade. A burst pipe, a backed-up main line or a water heater that has started leaking onto a finished floor does not wait for business hours, and it does not wait for a callback. The homeowner on the other end of that call is actively watching a problem get worse in real time.

That urgency changes the economics of a missed call. This guide shows how to estimate what unanswered calls are costing your plumbing business, why emergency calls deserve extra weight, which calls are most often lost and how to close the gap.

## Why plumbing is different

Most trades have a mix of urgent and planned work. Plumbing has an unusually large share of the first kind, and the cost of delay is visible and immediate. Water damage grows with time, so callers feel pressure and act on it.

Three consequences follow.

**Callers dial down the list.** An anxious homeowner does not leave a voicemail and wait. They call the next plumber, then the next.

**Answer speed decides the job.** The first company to answer, ask sensible questions and give an arrival window usually keeps the work.

**Reputation is built on the emergency.** The plumber who answered at 11pm becomes the family's plumber for years. The one who did not is forgotten.

## Put a number on it

To estimate what missed calls are really costing, three inputs matter: how many calls go unanswered in an average week, what share of those callers would have become paying jobs and your average ticket size. Multiply those together and extend it across a year, and most plumbing businesses find the number is larger than expected. It is often large enough to justify the exact kind of always-on call coverage that used to be affordable only to large multi-location operators.

### The three inputs

- **Unanswered calls per week.** Check your phone system's missed-call log if you have one.
- **Estimated close rate on inbound calls you do answer.**
- **Average ticket size** across your typical service mix.

### A worked example

These figures are illustrative, not benchmarks.

| Input | Example |
|---|---|
| Missed calls per week | 10 |
| Share that were real job opportunities | 50% |
| Opportunity calls per week | 5 |
| Close rate on answered opportunities | 50% |
| Jobs lost per week | 2.5 |
| Average ticket | $380 |
| Revenue lost per week | $950 |
| Revenue lost per year | about $49,000 |

Because plumbing tickets vary widely, from a $150 drain clearing to a multi-thousand-dollar repipe or water-heater replacement, use a blended average and then test the high and low ends. ## Emergency calls carry outsized weight

Not all missed calls are equal. A missed call about scheduling a routine drain cleaning is a lost job. A missed call about an active pipe burst is a lost job today and a lost customer relationship going forward, since that homeowner will remember who did, or did not, pick up when it mattered.

Weighing emergency calls more heavily in your estimate gives a more honest picture of what is at stake. One simple way is to split your missed calls into two groups, emergency and routine, and use a higher average ticket and a higher booking rate for the first group.

### Which plumbing calls are emergencies?

Agree on a written list. Typical entries include:

- Active flooding or a burst pipe that cannot be stopped
- Sewage backing up into the home
- No water to the whole house
- A leaking or failed water heater
- A gas smell near appliances, which should be directed to emergency services or the gas utility first

Everything else is urgent or routine, and it can follow a booking path. The same approach appears in our guides to [HVAC triage](/blog/hvac-emergency-call-triage) and [restoration calls](/blog/water-damage-restoration-first-hour-calls).

## Where plumbing calls get lost

The pattern is consistent across plumbing businesses.

### On the job

Your plumber is under a sink with a wrench, not holding a phone. This is the most common cause of misses, and it is a sign of a busy business, not a poorly run one.

### After hours

Many plumbing emergencies happen in the evening, when someone finally notices the water. Our [after-hours routing template](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) shows how to decide which calls wake someone.

### Peak periods

Freeze events, heavy rain and holiday weekends produce sudden spikes. The phones ring at the same moment your crews are busiest.

### Voicemail

Many callers do not leave a message, and those who do give incomplete details. For an active leak, voicemail is a poor tool. See [why every missed call is a lost job](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call).

## What to capture on a plumbing call

A short, consistent intake makes the job faster.

:::steps
1. "Is there water actively coming in, and can you reach the shutoff valve?"
2. "Do you smell gas, or is there any electrical hazard near the water?"
3. "Where is the problem: kitchen, bathroom, water heater, main line or outdoors?"
4. "When did it start, and is it getting worse?"
5. "What is the address, and the best number to reach you?"
6. "Can you text us a photo of the issue?"
:::

Question 2 is a safety screen. If there is a gas smell or an electrical hazard, the first instruction is to contact emergency services or the utility as appropriate, then escalate. The wording should be approved by your own safety lead. The structure resembles the one in our [call script guide](/blog/perfect-call-script-home-services).

## Closing the gap

You have three broad options, and many businesses combine them.

| Option | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Office staff | Relationship and judgment | Covers hours, not nights or surges |
| Answering service | Human voice at all hours | Per-minute cost and variable consistency |
| AI receptionist | Answers every call, runs the same questions, books and flags emergencies | Needs clear boundaries and escalation |

## Measure after you change something

Whatever you choose, measure the result after thirty days. Track answered share, after-hours answered share, booked jobs from answered calls and revenue from those jobs. If lost revenue falls and booked jobs rise, the change is paying for itself. If not, adjust the script or the coverage hours and test again.

## Seasonal patterns worth planning for

Plumbing demand has its own calendar. Freezing weather brings burst pipes, heavy rain brings backups and sump failures, and holidays bring overloaded drains and water heaters that fail at the worst moment. Look back at your own call log by month and mark the peaks. Then decide in advance who is on call, how many calls your phones can absorb at once and what you will say when the schedule is full. Honest wait times, a text with the arrival window and a clear emergency list protect your reputation during the weeks when everyone else is overwhelmed.

It also helps to keep a simple record of emergency calls you answered after hours and the jobs they produced. Those numbers make the value of coverage concrete when you decide whether to expand it, and they show your team why the on-call rotation matters.

:::takeaways
- Plumbing calls are unusually urgent, so answer speed often decides the job.
- Estimate lost revenue from missed calls, opportunity share, close rate and average ticket.
- Weigh emergency calls more heavily than routine ones.
- Most misses happen on jobs, after hours, during peaks and at voicemail.
- Use a short, consistent intake with a safety screen first.
- Cover the costliest hours first, then re-measure.
:::
