A restoration call has a clock on it that most home-service calls do not. A burst pipe, an overflowing washing machine or a failed water heater keeps adding damage until someone stops it and starts drying. The homeowner knows this, which is why they are not browsing reviews. They are dialing the first company that looks like it will pick up.

That makes restoration an unusually pure test of phone coverage. If you answer in thirty seconds and give a clear arrival window, you usually keep the job. If you send them to voicemail at 2:40am, they will be on the next number by 2:41. The principle is the same one behind [the cost of a missed call](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call), but here the average job is large, the urgency is real and the competition is local and persistent.

This guide covers how restoration companies can run the first sixty minutes of a call well: triage, intake, insurance, dispatch and follow-up.

## Why restoration phones are different

Three features make restoration calls unlike most others.

**The caller is stressed and short on time.** A homeowner standing in an inch of water is not in the mood for a long questionnaire. Questions must be short, in a sensible order, and clearly useful.

**The job type is not obvious.** A "wet carpet" call might be a plumbing leak, a roof leak, a washing-machine hose or groundwater. The category of loss changes the response and sometimes whether you or a plumber should go first.

**Insurance is almost always in the picture.** Unlike many trades, the person calling often does not control the budget. The insurer, a mortgage company or a landlord may be involved, and getting those details right at the start saves days later.

## Triage first: is this a danger, a loss or a leak?

Before capturing any detail, sort the call.

### Danger to people

Any report of fire, smoke, a person hurt, standing water around live electrical equipment, a strong gas smell, or structural collapse is a danger call. The first instruction is emergency services where appropriate, then your on-call escalation. As with [electrical triage](/blog/electrical-emergency-call-triage), the approved wording should come from your own safety lead.

### Active loss

Water is still entering the building, the source is not stopped, and damage is spreading. These calls get the fastest human response, ideally a technician on the phone or en route within your stated window.

### Contained or historical loss

The leak was fixed, the water is mostly gone, or the damage was found later, such as a stain or a musty smell. These need an assessment but not the first truck out. They go to the front of the day's schedule rather than the front of the night's.

:::tip
Agree internally what "active" means and write it down. A common source of friction is a call that the office treats as routine and the technician treats as urgent, or the reverse. A one-line definition prevents both.
:::

## The first-sixty-seconds question set

You want a short sequence that sorts the call, captures the essentials and ends with a clear next step.

:::steps
1. "Is anyone in danger, and is there any smoke, fire or electrical hazard?"
2. "Is water still coming in right now?"
3. "Do you know where it is coming from, and has it been stopped?"
4. "Which rooms or floors are affected?"
5. "When did it start?"
6. "What is the address, and can we reach you at this number?"
7. "Do you have homeowner's or renter's insurance, and have you contacted them?"
8. "Can you text us a few photos of the damage?"
:::

Notice how question 1 is the safety screen and question 2 is the classification. The rest build the lead record. If the source is a burst pipe, a separate plumber may need to be involved, and your intake should note that. ## Capturing insurance without giving coverage advice

The insurance conversation is where restoration calls most often go wrong. Callers want reassurance, and well-meaning staff give it. Resist that. A safe approach captures facts and stays out of coverage.

Capture:

- Whether the caller has insurance and the carrier's name.
- Whether a claim has been filed, and the claim number if available.
- Whether an adjuster has been assigned or visited.
- Whether the caller owns the property or rents, and if renting, the landlord's contact.

Say:

- "We will document the damage thoroughly so your insurer has what they need."
- "Coverage decisions are made by your insurance company, and we will not guess at them."

Do not say:

- "Your insurance will cover this."
- "You will not have to pay anything."

:::warning
Insurance and direct-billing rules differ by region, carrier and contract. Have your own counsel or program manager approve the wording your phones use about coverage, billing and work authorization before it goes live.
:::

## Dispatch windows and honest expectations

Restoration callers want a number. "A technician will be there in ninety minutes" is a promise, so only make the promises you can keep. A useful pattern is to give a window with a confirmation text, then update by text if it moves.

That requires knowing who is on call and how far away they are. For companies with a rotating on-call schedule, the answer to "who gets this call" should not depend on someone remembering. The [after-hours routing template](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) shows how to write that down, and [live escalation](/features/live-escalation) can bring a person into a call that needs one.

### What to text right after the call

A short confirmation message does a surprising amount of work:

- Your company name and the technician's arrival window.
- A link or instruction for sending photos.
- A reminder of anything the caller can safely do while waiting.
- Your main number for updates.

It also gives the caller something concrete to share with their insurer or family, which reinforces that they chose a professional operation. See how [SMS text-back](/features/sms-text-back) works for this kind of confirmation.

## Fire, smoke and mold calls

Water is the most common restoration call, but the same front door handles others.

**Fire and smoke.** These calls usually come after emergency services have already been involved. The questions change: is the property safe to enter, has the fire department cleared it, is the structure secured, who is the insurer, and is there a board-up need. The emotional tone matters; the caller has often lost far more than a wet carpet.

**Mold.** A mold call is often a slower, assessment-driven conversation. Capture where it was found, how long it has been visible, any known water history and any health concerns the caller mentions. Do not diagnose or reassure on the phone. Book an assessment.

**Storm and flood.** Large events bring a surge of calls at once, as roofers see in a [storm week](/blog/roofing-storm-call-surge-playbook). The same sorting rules apply, but your capacity becomes the constraint. Honest timing is more valuable than ever.

## How an AI receptionist supports restoration

An AI receptionist is a good fit for the front end of this workflow because the front end is repetitive and time-critical:

- It answers on the first ring at any hour.
- It runs the same safety screen and triage questions every time.
- It captures the complete lead record, including insurance details.
- It texts a confirmation and a photo request.
- It escalates danger and active-loss calls to a person.

:::takeaways
- Restoration calls are time-critical, and the first company to answer with a clear window usually wins.
- Sort calls into danger, active loss and contained loss before capturing detail.
- Use a short, fixed question set that ends with address, insurance and photos.
- Capture insurance facts and avoid coverage promises.
- Send a confirmation text with the arrival window and a photo request.
- Define "active" in writing and route on-call escalation by rule, not by memory.
:::

## What to measure

Track the numbers that reveal where the first hour leaks.

| Measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Calls answered live vs. total | How many chances you actually had |
| Time from call to dispatch decision | Whether the process is fast enough |
| Booked or dispatched share of answered calls | Whether the script converts |
| Jobs signed vs. dispatched | Whether on-site conversion is the issue |
| After-hours share of revenue | Whether night coverage is paying for itself |

## Final thought

Restoration is a business of trust under stress. The caller does not know you and needs to believe, in the first minute, that someone competent has the situation. A calm, structured, fast phone experience is the cheapest way to deliver that belief. Everything after the call depends on it.
