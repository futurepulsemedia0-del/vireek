Before comparing prices, it helps to be clear about what you are actually pricing. Answering the phone for a home service business can be handled three ways: hiring someone in-house, paying a traditional answering service or using an AI receptionist. Each has a very different cost structure, and the sticker price on any one of them only tells part of the story.

This guide walks through each option, shows how the costs behave as your call volume changes and explains the one number that actually decides the comparison. Prices below were published at the time of writing. Confirm current figures on each vendor's own site, including [Vireek's pricing page](/pricing).

## What a full-time receptionist costs

Industry salary data puts the average U.S. receptionist wage at roughly $17 to $18 an hour, which works out to a base salary in the $35,000 to $45,000 range once you annualize it. That is before payroll taxes, benefits or paid time off.

What that number buys is one person covering standard business hours. It does not cover nights, weekends, lunch breaks, sick days or the after-hours emergency call that home service businesses tend to lose the most money on.

### The costs that sit around the salary

- **Payroll taxes and benefits.** These vary by state and by employer. Ask your accountant for your real multiplier rather than guessing.
- **Training and ramp-up.** A new hire needs weeks to learn your services, area and scripts.
- **Management time.** Someone has to schedule, coach and cover them.
- **Turnover.** Front-desk roles often turn over, and each replacement restarts the learning curve.
- **Single-threaded capacity.** One person can answer one call at a time. A spike produces a queue.

A receptionist is valuable for relationships and tasks that need judgment. The question is whether the phone-answering part of the job is the best use of that salary, and whether it covers the hours where most missed calls happen.

## What a traditional answering service costs

Live answering services generally charge $50 to $500 or more a month depending on call volume and coverage hours. Per-minute overage rates commonly land between $0.75 and $3.00 a minute once you exceed your plan.

That cost scales in a straight line with how many calls you get. A slow month is cheap, but a busy season or a marketing push can raise the bill quickly, right when you need coverage the most.

### What you get for the money

A human agent answers, takes a message and sends it to you. Quality depends on the agents assigned to your account, who may not be dedicated to your business. For many businesses that is perfectly adequate. Our comparison of [AI receptionists and answering services](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-answering-service) covers consistency, cost scaling and what happens after the call in more detail.

## The number that actually decides the comparison

None of the three options is expensive or cheap in isolation. What makes the comparison worth running is what a missed call costs your specific business. A single missed emergency job in home services is commonly worth well over $1,000, and a contractor who misses even a handful of calls a week is very likely losing more in unbooked jobs than any of these options would cost to run every month.

:::stat
The right comparison is plan cost against lost revenue, not plan cost against plan cost.
:::

If you do not know your lost revenue, measure it. Our guide to [measuring your missed-call rate](/blog/how-to-measure-missed-call-rate-home-services) takes about an hour, and [why every missed call is a lost job](/blog/cost-of-a-missed-call) explains the logic behind the formulas.

### A quick break-even check

Suppose a plan costs $199 a month. If your average job is worth $450 and you close half of booked visits, one additional completed job roughly covers the monthly cost. Anything above that is margin. The point is not that every business will recover that much, but that the break-even is a small number of jobs, and you can test whether you reach it.

## Total cost of ownership: what to ask beyond the price

Sticker prices rarely tell the whole story. Ask every vendor, including us, about these items.

:::steps
1. What happens when I exceed my included minutes, and at what rate?
2. Are there setup, onboarding or integration fees?
3. How many seats, numbers and locations are included?
4. What is the contract length, and how do I cancel?
5. Which features are on which plan, and what changes if I upgrade mid-month?
6. How is data stored and secured, and who can access call content? See our [vendor security checklist](/blog/ai-vendor-security-checklist).
7. Can I test with real calls before committing?
:::

## Which option fits which business

There is no single right answer. A rough guide:

| Situation | Often a good fit |
|---|---|
| Few calls, mostly in office hours, strong front desk | Keep the receptionist; add after-hours coverage |
| Steady calls, many after hours, small team | AI receptionist, with human escalation for emergencies |
| Occasional calls, limited budget | Low-tier answering service or the AI free tier |
| Large seasonal spikes | AI receptionist, because capacity does not need pre-hiring |
| Complex calls needing judgment | Human staff, with AI handling intake and overflow |

Many businesses combine options: a person handles complex daytime calls while AI covers nights, weekends and overflow. Use the guide to [after-hours routing](/blog/after-hours-call-routing-template) to decide which calls go where.

## Hidden savings and hidden risks

Cost comparisons usually stop at the invoice, but two other effects matter. The first is recovered revenue: calls that used to go to voicemail now become bookings, which changes the net cost of the service. The second is risk: a plan that looks cheap but caps minutes can turn expensive in a busy month, and a plan with no cancellation path can lock you in. Read the overage terms, ask how minutes are counted (per call, per second, rounded up) and confirm what happens if you hit the cap during an emergency. Finally, set a calendar reminder to review the plan after ninety days. Your call volume, your seasons and your pricing may all have shifted, and the right tier is the one that fits the business you have now.

:::takeaways
- A receptionist's salary is the base cost, not the full cost, and it covers business hours only.
- Answering service costs scale in a straight line with call volume.
- AI receptionist plans include minutes at a flat monthly price, which makes spikes cheaper to absorb.
- Compare plan cost against your measured lost revenue, not against other plans.
- Ask about overage, setup fees, contract terms and data handling before signing.
- Test with real calls before committing.
:::
