Most home service owners do not think about call recording until they need it. A customer disputes what was promised, a technician wants to hear the original request, or a new AI tool starts transcribing every call. At that moment, the question of whether anyone was told becomes real.

This guide explains the basic framework in plain language, so you can ask better questions of your lawyer and your vendors. It is general information, not legal advice. Rules vary by state, country and situation, and they change. Treat everything here as a starting point for a conversation with a qualified professional.

## The basic framework: one-party and all-party consent

Federal law in the United States generally permits recording a phone call when at least one party to the call consents. That is often called one-party consent, and it is the starting point of the federal wiretap statute. A business that records its own calls is typically one of the parties.

Many states add their own rules, and a number of them require every party to the call to consent. The list is commonly cited as including states such as California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington, but the details differ, exceptions exist, and the list can change. Do not rely on a blog post, including this one, for the current rule in a particular state.

:::warning
A call can involve people in two different states. When one party is in an all-party consent state, the safest assumption is that the stricter rule may apply. Ask counsel how this applies to the areas you serve, not only the state where your office is.
:::

### What this means in practice

Because it is hard to know where every caller is, many businesses adopt the simplest conservative policy: tell every caller, at the start of every call, that the call may be recorded or transcribed. It costs a few seconds and removes a category of argument.

## Where AI receptionists add new questions

A traditional answering service or a human receptionist has always raised the recording question. An AI receptionist raises it more often, because transcription and summaries are a core part of how it works. Three separate questions are worth separating.

### 1. Is the call recorded, transcribed, or both?

Recording captures audio. Transcription captures text. Summaries capture a processed version of both. Each may be treated differently by your policy, your vendor's terms and applicable law. Know which ones your system creates by default, and which you can turn off.

### 2. Does the caller know they are talking to AI?

Expectations and rules about disclosing AI in customer interactions are developing in several places. Even where no rule applies to you, trust is the practical issue. A caller who feels tricked is a bad start to a customer relationship. Our [vendor security checklist](/blog/ai-vendor-security-checklist) lists transparency questions to ask, and Vireek's [responsible AI page](/responsible-ai) explains its approach.

### 3. Who can access it, and for how long?

A recording of a customer describing a leak is personal information. It may include an address, a name, a payment discussion or a family detail. Your policy should say who can listen, where it is stored and when it is deleted.

:::tip
Write the answers to those three questions on one page before you turn on any recording or transcription. A tool that is configured once, on purpose, is easier to defend than a tool whose defaults nobody reviewed.
:::

## The disclosure line: what to say and when

A recording notice should be short, early and clear. For example: "This call may be recorded or transcribed for quality and to help us serve you. If you would rather not be recorded, let us know." The exact words should be approved by your counsel, and they should match what your system really does.

### If a caller objects

Decide in advance what happens. Options include continuing without recording, transferring to a person, or offering a callback. The worst answer is no answer, where a staff member or system improvises.

### Keep the promise and the practice aligned

A notice that says "we do not record" while a system quietly keeps audio creates exactly the kind of risk a notice is meant to reduce. If your configuration changes, your wording must change with it.

## Text messages are a separate consent question

An AI receptionist often follows a missed or completed call with a text: a confirmation, a photo request, an arrival window. Text messaging to consumers is governed by its own rules in the United States, notably the Telephone Consumer Protection Act and related guidance, along with carrier requirements and state laws.

Points worth taking to your lawyer:

- What counts as consent for a transactional text that follows a customer's own call?
- What opt-out language must appear, and how is a STOP reply honored?
- Which messages are transactional and which are marketing, and do they need different consent?
- How long do you keep consent records?

The point is not to avoid texting, which customers generally appreciate, but to make sure the practice rests on a policy. See how [SMS text-back](/features/sms-text-back) is structured, and keep your own consent language reviewed.

## Retention and deletion

Recording policy is not only about the start of the call. It is also about the end of the data's life.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How long are recordings kept? | Longer retention increases exposure without always adding value |
| Can a customer request deletion? | Some privacy laws give people deletion rights |
| Who can export or share a recording? | Controls the risk of leaks and misuse |
| Is access logged? | Lets you investigate if something goes wrong |
| What happens when an employee leaves? | Prevents orphaned access |

For many home service businesses, the legitimate reasons to keep recordings are dispute resolution, quality review and training. A retention period tied to those purposes is easier to justify than "forever." Check how long disputes realistically take in your market and set the period with counsel.

## Questions to ask any AI receptionist vendor

Use this list in your next vendor conversation. A good vendor will answer them clearly and in writing.

:::steps
1. Is audio recorded, transcribed or summarized by default, and can each be turned off?
2. How is the recording notice delivered, and can I edit the wording?
3. How is the AI disclosed to callers, and can I configure it?
4. Where is data stored, for how long, and how is deletion handled?
5. Who at the vendor can access call content, and is access logged?
6. Which subprocessors handle call data, and where are they listed?
7. How are opt-outs and STOP replies handled for text messages?
8. Can you provide a data processing agreement and security documentation?
:::

## A practical rollout

Compliance work becomes manageable when it is broken into small, finishable steps.

### Week one: decide

Write the one-page policy: what you record, what you tell callers, how long you keep it, who can access it. Ask your counsel to review the notice wording and your text consent language.

### Week two: configure

Set the notice, the retention period and the access roles in your tools. Test the notice on a handful of calls, including a caller who objects.

### Week three: train

Make sure your staff know what the system says and what to do if a customer raises a concern. A policy nobody understands will be broken by accident.

### Quarterly: review

Check that settings still match the policy, review any complaints and revisit the policy when you add a new tool, state or service line.

:::takeaways
- Federal law generally allows recording with one party's consent, but several states require all parties to consent.
- Callers can be in a different state from you; the conservative approach is to notify every caller.
- AI adds three questions: what is recorded, whether the caller knows it is AI, and who can access the data.
- Text messages have their own consent rules; get your opt-in and opt-out language reviewed.
- Align your notice, your settings and your retention schedule so the promise matches the practice.
- Ask every vendor the same eight questions and keep the answers in writing.
:::

## A note on limits

None of this replaces professional advice. Laws differ between states and countries, they are updated, and they depend on facts that only you and your lawyer know. What this guide can offer is a structure for the conversation: decide what you record, tell people clearly, restrict access, delete on schedule and keep your words and your configuration in agreement.
